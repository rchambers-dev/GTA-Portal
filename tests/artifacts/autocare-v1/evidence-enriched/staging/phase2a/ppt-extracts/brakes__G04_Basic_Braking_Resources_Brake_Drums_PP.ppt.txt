The brake drum provides a friction surface for the brake linings to dissipate the heat and, in somecases, provide hub bearing or wheel location.

The drum fits over the wheel hub or the drive flange.

The wheel and the drum are bolted together and turn as one unit.

Drums sometimes have fins to aid cooling.

===== SLIDE 5 =====
Construction

A drum brake system comprises a backing plate, a drum, a wheel cylinder, two brake shoes, and springs.

A mechanism is often included to adjust the brake shoe position as the brake linings wear.

===== SLIDE 6 =====
Operation

A mechanically operated lever or a hydraulically operated piston pushes the brake shoes apart.

The harder the linings are forced against drum, the greater the friction and the slower the drum rotates.

When the brake linings contact the inside of the brake drum, friction occurs.

===== SLIDE 7 =====
Advantages

Drum brake systems self-energize.

The effect is that the brakes bite harder without any additional effort at the brake pedal.

This means that, as a shoe moves towards the rotating drum, it can be dragged into the friction surface.

===== SLIDE 8 =====
Disadvantages

The friction area of a brake drum is almost entirely covered by lining.

If overheating occurs, it can cause the braking efficiency to fall.

As a result, most of the heat must be conducted away through the drum to avoid overheating.

===== SLIDE 9 =====
Disadvantages

Water is also a problem for brakes because it accumulates in the drum.

A reduction in braking efficiency is called brake fade.

Water lowers the friction in the system and reduces the braking efficiency.
